## When Your Toy Hauler Is Your Home, Standard RV Insurance Falls Short

The full-time RV lifestyle has grown significantly in the past several years. Toy haulers are a popular choice for full-timers — you get a full living space plus the ability to bring ATVs, motorcycles, or gear for the destinations you're heading to.

But if you live in your toy hauler full-time, your standard RV policy is not designed for your situation. Standard RV insurance is built around the assumption that the RV is a second vehicle for recreational use — maybe 3–4 months of active use per year, and a home to return to between trips. When the toy hauler IS your home, every assumption in that standard policy breaks down.

## The Three Ways Standard RV Policies Fail Full-Timers

### Problem 1: Personal Property Limits Are Built for Campers, Not Residents

A camper needs to cover a weekend's worth of clothes, some cookware, camping chairs, and a portable speaker. That's $3,000–$5,000 of personal property.

A full-time resident has moved their entire life into the unit: furniture, kitchen equipment, a wardrobe for all seasons, electronics (laptop, TV, streaming devices), tools, sporting equipment, appliances, and whatever else makes up a household. The value of your personal property as a full-timer might be $20,000, $40,000, or more — far above the standard recreational-use policy limit.

If your toy hauler is burglarized or destroyed in a fire, the personal property payout under a standard policy doesn't begin to cover what you've lost.

### Problem 2: Liability Is Built for Occasional Campsite Visitors

When you camp for a weekend, your liability exposure is relatively limited — you're set up for a short time at a campsite, and the people who visit your site are a small number.

As a full-timer permanently or semi-permanently parked at an RV park or campground, you're effectively occupying a residence. You may have regular visitors, potentially employ help (housekeeping, maintenance), have packages delivered, and interact far more extensively with people coming to your site. The liability exposure looks much more like a homeowner's liability exposure than a weekend camper's.

Standard RV vacation liability is sized for the weekend camper. Full-timers need homeowners-equivalent liability — typically $100,000–$300,000 in personal liability coverage.

### Problem 3: Loss of Use Doesn't Account for Being Homeless

Standard RV emergency expense coverage pays for a few nights in a hotel if your RV breaks down and you can't stay in it. That's designed for an inconvenience, not a catastrophe.

If you're a full-timer and your toy hauler is destroyed or totaled, you've lost your home. You need to find housing while a claim is settled, a new unit is purchased, and repairs or replacement are arranged. That process takes weeks to months. Emergency lodging during that period is not a few nights' hotel — it's extended housing.

Full-timer loss of use coverage needs to reflect the actual cost of replacing your housing during the claim resolution period.

## What Full-Timer Endorsements Add

Full-timer endorsements are add-ons to your standard RV policy that address all three gaps:

**Increased personal property limits:** Typical full-timer endorsements bring personal property coverage to $20,000–$50,000 or more. You choose the limit based on the actual value of what you have in the unit.

**Homeowners-equivalent personal liability:** Full-timer liability endorsements provide $100,000–$300,000 in personal liability — the same coverage amounts you'd find on a homeowners or renters insurance policy.

**Medical payments to others:** Covers medical bills for visitors injured at your campsite, regardless of fault. Standard in homeowners policies; added via endorsement for full-timer RV coverage.

**Extended loss of use:** Full-timer loss of use endorsements provide daily benefits for significantly longer periods and at higher amounts than standard emergency expense coverage.

**Loss assessment:** If you're at a membership campground or RV resort that assesses damages after a covered incident, loss assessment coverage helps cover your share.

## Domicile State Considerations

Full-timers need a legal domicile state for vehicle registration, driver's license, and insurance purposes. The three most popular domicile states for full-timers are South Dakota, Texas, and Florida — each with advantages:

**South Dakota:**
- No state income tax
- Very friendly to full-timers with a simple domicile process
- One-day in-person process in Rapid City (Pennington County)
- Reasonable vehicle registration costs
- Annual vehicle registration renewal by mail

**Texas:**
- No state income tax
- Strong property rights culture (relevant to RV living)
- Can establish domicile via mail forwarding services without an in-person visit
- Higher vehicle registration costs than South Dakota but manageable

**Florida:**
- No state income tax
- Strong insurance industry, competitive RV insurance rates
- Can be done with a single in-person visit
- Higher vehicle registration costs; note that Florida is a named storm risk state for RV policies

From an insurance standpoint, we write coverage in all three states and can accommodate whichever domicile you choose. Note that your domicile state affects where your policy is written, which can affect coverage availability and price.

**Pro tip:** If you're establishing domicile, work with a full-timer mail forwarding service (America's Mailbox, Dakota Post, Escapees CARE) before selecting a state. The mail forwarding logistics and the actual domicile requirements both matter for the full process.

## What Full-Timers Still Need Separately

Full-timer endorsements to an RV policy do NOT cover:

**ATVs, UTVs, and motorcycles:** Same issue as non-full-timers — your recreational vehicles in the garage need their own OHV policies. Full-timer status doesn't change this.

**Vehicle coverage for your tow vehicle:** Your truck or SUV towing the toy hauler needs its own commercial or personal auto policy. Full-timer status doesn't change tow vehicle coverage.

**Health insurance:** RV insurance has nothing to do with health coverage. Full-timers often find that maintaining health insurance while moving between states requires specific plan types (marketplace plans or association plans that cover nationwide networks).

**Vehicle registrations in your domicile state:** Insurance and registration are separate processes. Maintaining your registration in your domicile state while traveling nationwide is a full-timer logistical requirement.

## Full-Timer Communities and Clubs

Two organizations are particularly relevant for full-timers considering both the lifestyle logistics and insurance:

**Escapees RV Club (SKP):** One of the largest and most established full-timer communities. Operates CARE (Continuing Assistance for Retired Escapees) — a supportive campground in Texas for members in need. Has a mail forwarding service and extensive resources for new full-timers.

**Full-Time Families:** Community focused on families full-timing with children. Resources, rallies, and community for traveling families.

Both provide community and practical resources that complement insurance planning.

## How Much Does Full-Timer Coverage Cost?

Full-timer coverage costs more than standard RV coverage due to the expanded coverage limits and the year-round use pattern.

**Estimated premium additions:**
- Personal property upgrade ($5K to $25K): +$80–$150/year
- Liability upgrade to homeowners-equivalent: +$100–$200/year
- Extended loss of use: +$50–$100/year
- Combined full-timer endorsement package: typically +$200–$500/year total

On a base fifth wheel policy of $650/year, full-timer coverage might bring the total to $850–$1,150/year. Given that the unit is your home, this is among the most cost-effective coverage decisions available.
